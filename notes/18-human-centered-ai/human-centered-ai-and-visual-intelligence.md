# Human-centered AI and visual intelligence

Human-centered AI asks not only what a model can predict, but whose needs it serves, what it fails to perceive, and how it affects people and society. The lecture organizes this perspective around three questions:

1. How can AI see what humans see?
2. How can AI help with what humans do not see?
3. What do humans actually want AI to see and do?

## Seeing what humans see

Object recognition was an important early goal because humans categorize objects rapidly despite changes in viewpoint, lighting, texture, background, and occlusion. Large datasets and deep learning produced major progress, but object labels alone are not complete visual understanding.

People also reason about relationships, intentions, activities, context, and events over time. A system that detects `person`, `horse`, and `hat` still has to understand who is riding what and who is wearing what.

## Seeing what humans do not see

Human perception is selective and fallible. Attention limits, change blindness, fatigue, and cognitive biases can cause important events to be missed. AI sensors may provide continuous monitoring or reveal patterns that are difficult for people to notice consistently.

This does not imply that more observation is always desirable. The same technology can create surveillance, expose private behavior, or amplify social bias.

## Seeing what humans want to see

Technical capability does not determine a worthwhile goal. Useful AI should begin with human needs, values, and well-being. That requires collaboration across machine learning, medicine, social science, design, policy, and the communities affected by a system.

The lecture's central direction is therefore:

> Build AI to augment individual and collective human capabilities, not merely to automate whatever can be measured.
